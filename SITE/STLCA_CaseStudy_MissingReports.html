In 2016, Seattle passed landmark legislation tying rent increases to housing code compliance — and embedded within it a mandatory accountability mechanism: an annual public report to the City Council on all eviction-related enforcement activity.

STLCA analyzed SDCI's own publicly available enforcement records across all seven City Council districts. What we found is that the accountability mechanism the Council designed has never activated — and that the data it would have surfaced tells a story the Council has never seen.

This case study documents what Ordinance 125054 required, what the enforcement record actually shows, and what happened when a member of the public formally asked SDCI to produce the missing reports.

Section 01 — The Mandate

The Law Is Clear

On June 6, 2016, the Seattle City Council passed Ordinance 125054 by a vote of 8 to 0. Sponsored in the Affordable Housing, Neighborhoods, and Finance Committee, the ordinance made it unlawful for property owners to raise housing costs on units that did not meet minimum maintenance standards under the Rental Registration and Inspection Ordinance. It transferred primary enforcement authority over the Housing Code to SDCI's Director, and it included a mandatory annual reporting requirement addressed directly to the City Council.

The Director shall report annually to the Affordable Housing, Neighborhoods and Finance Committee, or its successor committee, on all activity related to Section 22.206.180.J. This report shall include the number of inquiries from tenants and landlords, the number of inspections related to this provision, the outcome of those inspections, the number of housing cost increases delayed based on a determination by the department or refunds or credits required, and any interactions and information related to the Department's involvement in any eviction proceedings. To the extent feasible, the Director should obtain records on evictions in Seattle and review those records to determine if the Department conducted inspections at those locations related to Section 22.206.180.J and if the eviction resulted from application of this Section 22.206.180.J. The report shall be delivered to the Council by no later than June 15 of the following calendar year (beginning June 15, 2017). Please also provide the amount of the SDCI budget that is allocated toward the tenant landlord-inspections-enforcement regulations.
Ordinance 125054 · Section 9 Council Bill 118678 Passed 8–0 · June 6, 2016 Signed June 17, 2016 Effective July 10, 2016 First report due: June 15, 2017

The mandate was not ambiguous. It named a specific committee. It enumerated what to count. It required SDCI to proactively obtain eviction records from other agencies and cross-reference them against the department's own inspection history. And it set a clear deadline: the first report was due less than twelve months after the ordinance took effect.

The law has never been repealed. The reports have never been filed.

Section 02 — The Data

What the Reports Would Have Shown

STLCA analyzed SDCI's own complaint, inspection, and enforcement records spanning 2003–2025 — more than 233,000 records across all seven City Council districts. Within that dataset, 5,505 complaints contain eviction-related language in their description fields. The data below represents what nine years of required Director's Reports should have placed before the City Council.

Eviction Complaints Closed Without Documented Inspection — By Year
Percentage of closed/completed eviction-keyword complaints with no inspection date or result in SDCI records. Ordinance 125054 took effect July 10, 2016. The first Director's Report was due June 15, 2017 and was not filed.

Before 2016, SDCI was inspecting between 17% and 57% of eviction-related complaints before closure — inadequate, but reflecting some field engagement with tenants. Beginning in 2018 — the year after the first report was due and not filed — the uninspected closure rate crossed 87% and never recovered.

By 2021, only 5 of 403 closed eviction complaints had any inspection on record — a rate of 98.8%. In 2025, as complaint volume reached an all-time high of 709, the uninspected closure rate was 97.7%. The Council passed new tenant protections throughout this period without knowing any of this.

Section 03 — The Mechanism

What "Completed" Does and Doesn't Mean

The same database that reliably records inspection dates when inspections occur leaves those fields blank on thousands of closed eviction cases. A blank is not a missing entry. It is the record.

The vast majority of eviction complaints in SDCI's system carry a status of "Completed" or "Closed." In many cases, the description field contains only a notice type, a code section citation, or a certification form name. These records reflect a closure process that does not require a field inspector to visit the property, speak with a tenant, or document conditions on the ground.

In numerous records across all seven districts, descriptions indicate that a property owner submitted a certification form — attesting, for instance, that no displacement occurred, or that a notice was recorded under a specific code section. The case is marked complete. No one verifies the certification independently.

This is not a criticism of individual case managers. It is a description of a system operating without the oversight mechanism the City Council built in 2016. The annual reporting requirement was designed precisely to surface how often this was happening and whether it was consistent with the law's intent.

The ordinance specifically required SDCI to cross-reference eviction records from other agencies against its own inspection history — to determine whether the department was present before an eviction occurred. That work has never been done. SDCI confirmed this when formally asked.

Section 04 — The Response

What SDCI Said When Asked

In 2025, a Seattle tenant submitted a public records request to SDCI specifically asking for the Director's Reports required under Ordinance 125054. The request also asked for a budget breakdown showing how much of SDCI's allocation went toward tenant-landlord inspections, enforcement, and regulations.

In response to the request for the Director's Reports, the records office provided a single-page budget summary. No reports were produced — because, as SDCI subsequently confirmed in writing, no reports had ever been prepared.

SDCI · Code Compliance Division · Written Communication · March 2025
"On the report you are asking for, we've never prepared such a report. I checked with our team and our (now retired) director when you first made the request. I'm copying our public disclosure coordinator since you raise the issue, but it doesn't appear we missed anything."

"I note that 22.206.180.J doesn't exist in the code. It makes a jump from I to K. I've made a note to flag that for the City Code Reviser since it looks like an error. The items you mention look like they are related to 22.206.180.I, which details a process to prevent a rent increase when housing conditions are not met. We get very few calls under that section of code."
STLCA Analysis

The subsection numbering gap cited in this response is a consequence of subsequent amendments to SMC 22.206.180 — not evidence that the reporting obligation does not exist. Section 9 of Ordinance 125054 created a standalone reporting duty that does not depend on the subsection surviving intact through later code renumbering. The ordinance that created the obligation has never been repealed.

The determination that "it doesn't appear we missed anything" was made unilaterally by the office the reporting requirement was designed to hold accountable — without a City Attorney legal opinion and without notification to the City Council that the mandate had gone unfulfilled for eight years at the time of writing.

The response also confirms that SDCI has never attempted the cross-reference of eviction records that the law specifically directed it to perform. The tenant who submitted the request had researched this at their own expense, without subpoena power or institutional resources, and produced more accountability documentation than SDCI had over nine years of legal obligation.

Note on the RRIO Annual Reports: SDCI began publishing RRIO Annual Reports in 2022. Those reports satisfy a separate and distinct obligation under Ordinance 124011, Section 17, as amended by Ordinance 125343 — covering registration status, inspection pass/fail rates, and inspector audits. They do not contain a single data element required by Ordinance 125054, Section 9: no count of rent increase delays, no refunds or credits ordered, no eviction cross-referencing, no budget breakdown for tenant-landlord enforcement. The two obligations arise from different ordinances, cover different program activities, and are not interchangeable. The RRIO reports are not a substitute for the reports this ordinance required.

Section 06 — Findings

STLCA Findings

1

The Reporting Mandate Is Real and Has Never Been Repealed

Ordinance 125054, Section 9, created a mandatory annual reporting obligation with the first report due June 15, 2017. The ordinance passed 8–0. It has not been repealed. Subsequent renumbering of SMC 22.206.180 subsections does not extinguish a duty created by an unrepealed ordinance.

2

SDCI Has Filed Zero of Nine Required Reports

No annual Director's Report covering enforcement activity under SMC 22.206.180 has been delivered to the City Council for any year from 2016 through 2025. SDCI confirmed this in writing in March 2025, characterizing it as not appearing to have "missed anything."

3

The Data Those Reports Would Have Contained Is Deeply Troubling

STLCA's independent analysis shows that 85.3% of eviction-related complaints were closed without a documented field inspection — a rate that worsened every year from 2018 through 2025, reaching 97.7% last year. The City Council has been legislating tenant protections for nine years without access to this enforcement data.

4

SDCI Made a Unilateral Determination That the Obligation Did Not Apply

The determination that the reporting mandate did not exist was made by the office the oversight requirement was designed to hold accountable — without a City Attorney legal opinion, and without any notification to the City Council that the mandate had gone unfulfilled for eight consecutive years.

5

The Council Has No Basis to Evaluate Whether the 2016 Law Is Working

The reporting requirement was the accountability spine of Ordinance 125054. Without the reports, the Council has never been able to assess whether landlords are raising rents on code-deficient units, whether field inspections are reaching tenants who need them, or whether evictions are occurring at properties SDCI has already flagged. The law exists in the code. Its enforcement accountability does not exist in the record.

6

The RRIO Annual Reports Do Not Satisfy This Obligation

SDCI began publishing RRIO Annual Reports in 2022 under a separate legal authority: Ordinance 124011, Section 17, as amended by Ordinance 125343. Those reports cover program-level RRIO metrics (registration renewals, inspection pass/fail rates, private inspector audits) and were themselves filed six years after their own first deadline. They share no data elements with the report Ordinance 125054 required. Any assertion that the RRIO annual reports satisfied the Ordinance 125054 obligation conflates two distinct legal mandates from two different ordinances covering two different subject matters. The obligation created by Ordinance 125054, Section 9, has never been met.

What This Case Study Demonstrates

This is not a case of an agency struggling to enforce a difficult law. It is a case of a mandatory accountability mechanism — specifically designed to prevent enforcement failures from happening invisibly — going unfulfilled for nine consecutive years.

The City Council passed Ordinance 125054 because it understood that rent increases should not be permitted on units with unresolved code violations, and that the public deserved to know how often they were. The annual report was not optional guidance. It was the provision by which the Council would know whether the law's protections were reaching the people they were designed to protect.

STLCA's analysis shows that eviction complaint volume in Seattle increased 253% between 2016 and 2025 — from 90 complaints to 709 — while the rate of documented inspections on those complaints collapsed from 52% to 2.3%. The oversight mechanism designed to detect that collapse was never activated.

The question for the City Council is not whether the reports were missed. They were. The question is what the data — now in the public record — should change about how Seattle enforces its tenant protection laws going forward.

All enforcement data in this report is derived from the City of Seattle's publicly available Complaints & Violations Built Environment dataset (Seattle Open Data Portal), covering records 2003–2025 across all seven City Council districts. Eviction-related complaint identification uses keyword analysis of description fields across 226,306 records with non-null descriptions. Inspection verification requires both LastInspDate and LastInspResult fields to be populated on closed/completed records. Ordinance 125343 (RRIO Housing Code Update, 2017) reviewed for distinction between SMC 22.206.180 reporting obligation and Ordinance 124011 Section 17 RRIO program reporting. SDCI response cited from written communication obtained through public records process, March 2025. No individual tenant names, complaint identification numbers linked to personal information, or private residential addresses are disclosed in this report.
