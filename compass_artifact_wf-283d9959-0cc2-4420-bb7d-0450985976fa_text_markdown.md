# Seattle tenant protections: a legislative cascade from 2010 to 2025

**Seattle enacted more than 20 major tenant protection laws between 2010 and 2025, with the heaviest legislative activity concentrated in 2016–2017 and 2020–2021** — periods that align closely with the SDCI complaint volume inflection points you identified. The city went from a relatively light regulatory framework (just cause eviction since 1980, basic rent increase notice since 1983) to one of the most tenant-protective regimes in the country, layering registration requirements, screening restrictions, fee caps, eviction defenses, and relocation mandates in rapid succession. Washington State added a parallel layer of protections in 2021 and 2025, compounding compliance obligations and likely driving further complaint and enforcement activity.

---

## The foundational layer: RRIO and legacy protections (2010–2014)

Seattle's modern tenant protection architecture begins with laws predating this timeline but still operationally active throughout it. The **Just Cause Eviction Ordinance** (SMC 22.206.160, enacted **1980**) required landlords to cite one of roughly 16 enumerated reasons to terminate a month-to-month tenancy. The **Rental Agreement Regulation Ordinance** (RARO, Ordinance 111768, **1983**, SMC 7.24) mandated **60 days' notice for rent increases of 10% or more** — far exceeding the state's 30-day baseline — and banned one-way leases. A **1989 amendment** to Seattle's Open Housing Ordinance (SMC 14.08) prohibited discrimination against Section 8 voucher holders, and the **Tenant Relocation Assistance Ordinance** (TRAO, **1990**, SMC 22.210) required relocation payments for low-income tenants displaced by demolition or substantial rehabilitation.

The first major new law of the 2010s was the **Rental Registration and Inspection Ordinance (RRIO)**, passed in **fall 2012** as **Ordinance 124011** (SMC 22.214). RRIO required every rental property in Seattle to register with the city and undergo inspections every 5–10 years for habitability compliance. Registration rolled out in phases: properties with **10+ units by July 2014**, **5–9 units by March 2015**, and **1–4 units by December 2016**. Crucially, RRIO made non-registration a defense to eviction and established penalties of **$150/day** for the first 10 days and **$500/day** thereafter. The phased registration schedule alone likely contributed to rising SDCI complaint volumes starting in 2014–2015, as tens of thousands of previously unregistered units entered the regulatory system.

In **2015**, the city amended the TRAO to strengthen the connection between large rent increases and relocation assistance, prohibiting landlords from evicting tenants after filing for a tenant relocation license and linking rent increases exceeding **10%** to the relocation framework.

## The 2016–2017 wave that reshaped landlord-tenant relations

The period from late 2016 through 2017 represents the single densest burst of new tenant regulation in Seattle's history — four major ordinances in roughly 14 months — and corresponds directly to the sharp uptick in SDCI complaint volume you observed starting around 2015.

**Ordinance 125114** (Council Bill 118755), signed by Mayor Murray in **August 2016** with enforcement beginning **January 2017**, was a sweeping package. It **expanded source of income discrimination protections** beyond Section 8 vouchers to cover Social Security, SSI, unemployment benefits, child support, refugee assistance, and any government or nonprofit benefit program. It required landlords to subtract subsidy payments when calculating income-to-rent ratios, banned preferred-employer discount programs (targeting Amazon/Microsoft arrangements), and mandated that screening criteria be disclosed upfront. Most controversially, this ordinance created the **First-in-Time Rule** (SMC 14.08.050), requiring landlords to screen applications chronologically and offer tenancy to the first qualified applicant — effectively banning tenant selection among equally qualified candidates. The First-in-Time Rule was immediately challenged in *Yim v. City of Seattle* and was **unanimously upheld by the Washington Supreme Court in November 2019**.

Just four months later, in **December 2016**, the Council unanimously passed **Ordinance 125222** (CB 118817, effective January 2017), imposing **move-in fee limits**. Security deposits plus non-refundable fees were capped at **one month's rent**; non-refundable fees alone at **10% of one month's rent**; and pet deposits at **25%**. The ordinance also created **installment payment rights**: tenants on leases of six months or longer could spread deposits across six equal installments. Landlords who overcharged faced damages of **double the unlawful amount** plus attorney's fees.

In **August 2017**, the Council passed **Ordinance 125393** (CB 119015), the **Fair Chance Housing Ordinance** (SMC 14.09), effective **February 2018**. This **prohibited landlords from screening applicants based on criminal history** — including arrests, convictions, and pending charges — with narrow exceptions for registered sex offenders (requiring individualized assessment) and certain federally regulated housing. Penalties reached **$55,000 per violation** by the third offense. This was among the most restrictive criminal background screening bans for housing in any U.S. city.

Together, these four laws (source of income expansion, first-in-time, move-in fee caps, and fair chance housing) fundamentally restructured the landlord-tenant screening and financial relationship, generating new compliance categories and complaint vectors that would logically drive SDCI enforcement volume upward starting in 2017–2018.

## Incremental expansions and the pre-COVID ramp (2018–2019)

The 2018–2019 period saw relatively fewer blockbuster ordinances at the city level but introduced important incremental expansions. **Washington State** passed **RCW 59.18.255** in **2018**, extending source of income discrimination protections statewide — a move that reinforced Seattle's existing framework but expanded awareness and complaints.

In **2019**, the state legislature passed **SSB 5600**, extending the pay-or-vacate notice period from **3 days to 14 days** and requiring a statewide **60-day notice for all rent increases** (RCW 59.18.140), aligning state law with what Seattle already required for large increases.

At the city level, the **Roommate Ordinance** (**Ordinance 125950**, SMC 7.24.031–032, **October 2019**, effective July 2020) required landlords to accept immediate family members as additional occupants without screening and allowed non-family roommates to assume the lease if the original tenant departed. This ordinance also prohibited landlords from charging domestic violence survivors for property damage caused by their abusers.

## The 2020–2021 legislative explosion and SDCI complaint surge

The period from early 2020 through late 2021 produced the most intense concentration of tenant protections in Seattle's history — at least **10 major ordinances or executive actions in under two years** — and aligns with the post-2018 complaint volume explosion in your SDCI data.

The **Winter Eviction Ban** (**Ordinance 126041**, **February 10, 2020**) was the first domino. Passed 7-0 and sponsored by Councilmember Sawant, it created a defense to eviction between **December 1 and March 1** for tenants earning below 100% of area median income. Landlords with four or fewer units were exempt. This was described as the first law of its kind nationally.

Weeks later, **Mayor Durkan's COVID-19 Emergency Order** (March 2020) imposed a blanket **eviction moratorium**, prohibiting all residential evictions except for imminent health and safety threats. The moratorium was extended repeatedly through 2021 and did not fully expire until **February 28, 2022** under Mayor Harrell, with a six-month post-moratorium defense period extending protections through approximately mid-2022. **Governor Inslee's Proclamation 20-19** (March 18, 2020) imposed a parallel statewide moratorium that ran through **October 31, 2021**.

The Council then passed a rapid series of COVID-era emergency ordinances in **May 2020**:

- **Ordinance 126075** (CB 119784, May 6, 2020): Created a defense to eviction for non-payment during or within six months after the moratorium and prohibited late fees and interest during the emergency
- **Ordinance 126077** (CB 119787, May 15, 2020): Banned landlords from using eviction history that occurred during the emergency period as grounds to deny future tenancy
- **CB 119788** (May 2020): Entitled tenants to installment repayment plans for rent accrued during the moratorium

**2021** brought even more sweeping changes. In **March 2021**, the Council passed **CB 120007** establishing a **Right to Counsel** (SMC 22.206.195) — guaranteeing free legal representation for any Seattle renter facing an eviction lawsuit, regardless of income. Seattle became the eighth U.S. city with such a guarantee.

In **June 2021**, two additional landmark measures passed. The **School Year Eviction Ban** (**Ordinance 126369**, CB 120046) created a defense to eviction during the school year for households with children and school employees. The **Right of First Refusal** ordinance (CB 120090, SMC 7.24.030.J) closed a long-standing loophole by requiring landlords to offer lease renewals **60–90 days before expiration** on reasonable terms, effectively extending just cause protections to fixed-term lease tenants.

In **September 2021**, the Council passed two of its most consequential measures. **CB 119585** extended the rent increase notice period to **180 days** for any housing cost increase — the longest such requirement of any major U.S. city, up from 60 days for large increases. The **Economic Displacement Relocation Assistance (EDRA)** ordinance (CB 120173, SMC 22.12, effective **July 1, 2022**) required landlords imposing rent increases of **10% or more** to fund relocation assistance equal to **three months of current housing costs** for income-eligible tenants (≤80% AMI).

At the state level, **2021** was equally transformative. **E2SSB 5160** (signed April 2021) made Washington the **first state to guarantee right to counsel** in eviction cases, established the Eviction Resolution Pilot Program (ERPP) requiring pre-filing mediation, and mandated repayment plans for pandemic-era arrears. **ESHB 1236** (signed May 2021) established **statewide just cause eviction protections** (RCW 59.18.650), bringing the entire state in line with what Seattle had maintained since 1980.

## Post-moratorium adjustments and the current regulatory regime (2022–2025)

Seattle's eviction moratorium expired **February 28, 2022**, with post-moratorium defenses running through mid-2022. The **Court of Appeals** in *RHAWA v. City of Seattle* (March 2022) upheld the Winter Eviction Ban and installment payment ordinance but **struck down** the blanket six-month post-moratorium eviction defense on due process grounds for not requiring tenants to prove financial hardship.

In **April 2023**, the Council passed the **Late Fee Cap** (**Ordinance 126803**, CB 120541, SMC 7.24.034), limiting late rent fees to **$10 per month** and banning fees for notice preparation or delivery — effectively eliminating a significant revenue tool landlords had used.

In **June 2025**, Seattle passed a ban on **algorithmic rent-pricing software** (CB 121000), prohibiting landlords from using coordinated price-setting tools like RealPage. Penalties reach **$7,500 per violation**. At the state level, **HB 1217** (signed May 2025) established **Washington's first rent stabilization law**, capping increases at **7% plus CPI or 10%, whichever is lower**, with 90-day notice requirements, a 12-year new construction exemption, and a 15-year sunset clause. **HB 1003** (effective July 2025) updated eviction notice procedures to require specific vacate dates and certified mail service.

## Connecting the legislative timeline to SDCI complaint patterns

The complaint volume trajectory you described — a sharp rise starting **2015** and explosion after **2018** — maps cleanly onto three legislative acceleration phases. The 2014–2016 RRIO registration rollout brought roughly **160,000 rental units** into a compliance system for the first time, mechanically increasing the surface area for violations and complaints. The 2016–2017 cluster of four major ordinances (source of income, first-in-time, move-in fees, fair chance housing) created entirely new categories of violations that SDCI was charged with enforcing, while simultaneously increasing tenant awareness of their rights. The 2020–2021 legislative avalanche — at least ten ordinances layered atop COVID emergency orders — generated an unprecedented compliance burden and corresponding enforcement demand. Each new protection both empowered tenants to file complaints and created new grounds on which complaints could be filed, producing the compounding effect visible in SDCI data. The regulatory framework has continued expanding through 2025, with algorithmic pricing bans and statewide rent caps adding further compliance dimensions.
